The job

Decide what a diagram shows, and why, before anything is drawn.

diagrams is the skill that does the work you cannot see in a finished diagram: who it is for, what it is supposed to let them do, what it covers, what it deliberately leaves out, and how it will be read. Only then does it choose a type, and only then does it hand the result to something that draws.

It is render-agnostic, and that is the whole design. The vocabulary it produces — shapes, containers, connectors, brackets, axes, lanes, overlaps, labels, a key — is the same whether it ends up on a local tldraw canvas, on a shared board, or in a Mermaid block inside a markdown file. Swapping the tool changes nothing about whether the diagram works.

A diagram is a visual representation that helps someone. Two conditions, both required. Most things called diagrams pass the second one and fail the first.

01 · The claim

Approved on Tuesday. Unused by Friday.

Everyone nodded at the diagram in the meeting. A week later nobody had used it. The drawing was fine — aligned, legible, well spaced. It failed four decisions earlier, before anything was drawn: who it was for, what they were meant to do afterwards, what it deliberately left out, and where it would be read.

None of those four are visible in a finished diagram. That is exactly why they get skipped, and why the failure always looks like a drawing problem.
02 · The definition

Two conditions, and the first one is the hard one.

A diagram is a visual representation that helps someone. Both conditions are required: it has to help someone — even if that someone is you — and it has to be visually represented. An org chart nobody opens, a photographed whiteboard, six boxes on a slide: all pass the second gate. Failing the first is not something a renderer can fix.

03 · Intention

Write the sentence before you draw the thing.

The diagram helps this audience to do this one thing. Fill in both blanks first. “Make me a diagram” fills in neither — it names a medium, not an intention. The sentence has a second job too: it tells you when a table, an email or a single sentence would do the work better. Sometimes the honest answer is that the right artifact is not a diagram.

Scale is the fourth of these decisions and the quietest. Size times interaction mode: a poster, a worksheet, a slide and a wall are four different diagrams of the same subject, and only one of them is the one you are making.
04 · Audience

Same subject. Two diagrams.

A diagram is only good in the eye of the beholder. Someone who lives a process describes it in three steps and their own words; someone who builds it needs state names, rules and the parts that fail. That is two diagrams, not one diagram at two zoom levels. Picking one audience is what makes every later decision decidable.

05 · Scope

Say what is out. Then stop early.

Write what it covers and what it does not, out loud, before drawing. Then build in importance order: essentials, elaboration, context. Detail earns its place by serving the intention — most of what gets added is added because somebody had it, not because a reader needed it.

06 · The type

Not the word they used. What they are stuck on.

People say “flowchart” for anything with a box in it. Pick the type from the centre of the stuck-ness instead: time, arrangement, or context. Twelve recipes cover almost everything between them, and they are a grammar rather than a set of templates. When none of them fit, adapt the nearest one — and say which one you adapted.

07 · The key

A growing key is a symptom, not a fix.

A key holds three things: the visual vocabulary, the sources and timeframe, and a note of intention. The moment it explains what a colour means, it has become a decoder ring — and every row in it is a label that belonged on the drawing. Colour follows the same rule: if it carries meaning, something else must carry it too.

08 · The render

The tool was never the variable.

Shapes, containers, connectors, brackets, axes, lanes, overlaps, labels, a key. That is what the earlier decisions produce, and none of it changes when the tool does. A local canvas, a shared board, or a Mermaid block all render the same diagram. Picking the tool first is picking the only part that changes nothing.

09 · The payoff

The only test that counts has a person in it.

Four decisions with no drawing in them, then four with drawing, then a loop. There is no right or wrong in diagramming; there is only better, and on purpose. Which means the test is not whether you like it, and not whether it passes a checklist. Show it to the person it was for and watch what they do with it.

The twelve recipes

Sorted by what the reader is stuck on.

A grammar for building diagrams, not a catalogue to pick from by appearance. Four of these — flows, sitemaps, concept models, journey maps — are deep enough to be skills of their own; the rest live here as recipes.

Gantt

Centre: time. Parallel work across teams, and where the overlaps actually are. Adapt it for a roadmap or a timeline.

Swim lane

Centre: time. Hand-offs between teams or systems. The lane boundary is the point of the diagram; the steps are secondary.

Journey

Centre: time. One actor, experience and feeling over time. Contexts as rows, phases as columns.

Flow

Centre: time. One actor, logic and data. Journey is experience; flow is decisions.

Hierarchy

Centre: arrangement. Levels and ownership. Horizontal brackets to two levels, vertical at three or more.

Schematic

Centre: arrangement. A physical interface drawn in place — where things sit relative to each other in the real thing.

Block

Centre: arrangement. A concrete assembly and the pieces it is made of.

Concept

Centre: arrangement. Abstract things and the labelled relationships between them. Almost every line needs a verb on it.

Sign

Centre: context. Cause and effect as couplets, each marked + or −. Loops become visible on their own.

Continuum

Centre: context. Opposing views along one axis. Useful precisely when people think they disagree more than they do.

Quadrant

Centre: context. Two axes, four regions. The axes are the argument; the dots are evidence for it.

Venn

Centre: context. Combinations and overlaps. The interesting cell is usually the empty one.

Where it stops

Some things are not diagrams.

A chart is not a diagram. A funnel, a bar chart, a distribution — those answer how much, and the skill is told to say so rather than draw a picture of numbers. A wireframe is a screen. A breadboard is places and affordances in words. Three unchosen options drawn side by side is a decision that has not been made yet, wearing a picture.

What is left is the thing this is actually for: someone is stuck on how parts of something relate, and a drawing would unstick them. Everything above is about earning the right to make that drawing.

Method provenance

Where this one comes from.

Two books, and an adaptation. The books are the method; the adaptation is what it took to make it survive shipping to someone else’s machine.

Abby Covert

Stuck? Diagrams Help. The definition, the eight moves, the twelve recipes, the craft principles, and the creed the skill runs on — “there is no right or wrong in diagramming; there is only better and on purpose.” The insistence that a diagram is judged in the eye of the beholder is hers, and it is the load-bearing idea here.

Read about the book

Dan Brown

Communicating Design, chapters 1 and 2. The three layers and the stopping rule that comes with them, the conventions for showing as-is against to-be, and the meeting recipe for walking a group through a diagram without turning it into a performance. “You are a journalist, not a joke-teller.”

What shipping changed

The skill was vendored from a personal library where nine sibling skills existed. Every hand-off to one of them became conditional — use the specialised skill if it is installed, otherwise use the recipe and say which. The renderer split was rewritten around a local canvas, and the failure modes became a real Anti-patterns section.
