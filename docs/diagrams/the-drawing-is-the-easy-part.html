This is the article about diagrams, the skill that decides what a diagram shows before anything is drawn. It sits one step upstream of anything that draws, and it writes its decisions down.

diagrams is the discipline that decides what a diagram shows and writes it down. Four decisions come before a single box, and they, not the tool, decide whether the picture works.

01 · The claim

The claim.

Everyone approved the diagram in the meeting. A week later nobody had opened it again. The lines were straight, the boxes aligned, the labels legible. It had already failed four decisions before anyone drew a box.

02 · The definition

The definition.

A diagram is a visual representation that helps someone. Both conditions have to hold, and the first one is where diagrams fail. A tidy drawing that helps no one still clears the easy gate. It was never going to work, and the rendering had nothing to do with it.

03 · Intention

Intention.

Write one sentence before anything else. The diagram helps this audience to do or decide this thing. If you cannot fill the blanks, you are not ready to draw. Sometimes the finished sentence shows that an email would do the job better.

Scale, the fourth decision the scenes compress. Scale is size times interaction mode, and a poster, a worksheet, a slide and a wall are four different diagrams of the same subject.
04 · Audience

Audience.

A diagram is only good to the person reading it. Someone who lives a process and someone who builds it need different words and different depth. Two readers means two diagrams. Pick one reader and every later choice has an answer.

05 · Scope

Scope.

Say what the diagram covers and what it leaves out, before drawing. Then build in importance order. The essentials come first, the elaboration second, the context last. When you are unsure, ship the essentials and stop.

Choosing the type from what the reader is stuck on rather than the word they used. Time, arrangement, or context, across twelve recipes that behave as a grammar. Some of those types — flows, sitemaps, concept models, journey maps — run deep enough to be skills of their own.
The key as the tell. A key holds the visual vocabulary, the sources and timeframe, and a note of intention. When it starts explaining what a colour means or decoding an abbreviation it has become a decoder ring, which is a sign the diagram is underlabelled. Any colour that carries meaning needs a second cue behind it, because contrast has to survive colour-blindness.
06 · The render

The render.

By now the decisions have produced a small vocabulary of shapes, containers, connectors, labels, and a key. A local canvas, a shared board, or a Mermaid block all draw the same diagram from it. Picking the tool first picks the one part that was never the variable.

Where this sits against the neighbours. Not problem-framing, not shaping, not wireframes, not breadboarding — and what it declines to draw, including a chart of numbers and a decision nobody has made yet.
07 · The payoff

The payoff.

Four decisions come before any drawing, and four come with it. The order decides whether the diagram works, and the tool does not. There is no right or wrong in diagramming, only better and on purpose. So show it to the person it was for and watch whether they use it.

The eight moves

Four decisions, then four with the pen.

The first four have no drawing in them at all. They are the ones a finished diagram cannot show and every reader feels. Then loop back and test it — critique is not the test; a reader is.

1 · Intention

The diagram helps [audience] to [do or decide X]. If a table or an email would deliver it better, write that instead.

2 · Audience

What they know and whether they live it or build it. If the person stuck is you, that is a valid audience.

3 · Scope

It will cover this and not cover that. Goes wrong three ways: too much, too little, or the wrong register.

4 · Scale

Size times interaction mode. A poster, a worksheet, a slide and a wall are four different diagrams of the same thing.

5 · Shapes

The objects: people, places, things. Shapes and lines are a loop, not two separate steps.

6 · Lines

The relationships. Arrange the shapes and imagine the lines before committing them.

7 · Labels

They have to speak for themselves. Under about 25 characters, consistent casing, matched to the intention.

8 · Key

Vocabulary, sources and timeframe, a note of intention. Nothing else, or it becomes a decoder ring.

The payoff

The order decides. The tool does not.

Intention, audience, scope and scale decide whether a diagram works. The render is the last and cheapest step. You leave with the eight moves and one test you can run today: show it to the person it was for and watch whether they use it.
