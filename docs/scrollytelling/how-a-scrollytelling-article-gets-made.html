A scrollytelling article can move a great deal and explain nothing. How do you make one that actually explains? By treating the writing as the work and the motion as the last step.

The method

A scrolling article, and the skill that builds it.

A scrollytelling article is a long web page where a graphic stays fixed on one side while short blocks of text scroll past on the other. Each block changes the graphic to one new state, so the picture and the words advance together. You have read them in the New York Times and on The Pudding.

An agent skill is a folder of instructions an AI coding agent loads on demand. It carries the steps, the rules, and the checks for one kind of task, so the agent does that task the same way each time.

scrollytelling is one of eight product design skills in this library. It turns a decision that already exists into a single standalone HTML article, and this page is one it produced about itself.

00 · An article that moves

An article that moves.

A scrollytelling article can move a great deal and still explain nothing. This skill treats the writing as the work and the motion as the last step. It runs in two phases, and it builds nothing until the script is approved.

01 · Two phases and a gate

Two phases and a gate.

The skill runs in two phases in a fixed order. Phase one writes a scene-by-scene script. Phase two builds a single standalone HTML file. A gate sits between them, and only your approval opens it.

02 · Goal and tone first

Goal and tone first.

Before any drafting, you pick one goal and one tone from a menu. The goal decides what the reader can do at the end. The tone decides how it sounds. Asked as open questions these get vague answers, so the skill lists the options and recommends one of each.

A goal and a tone can fight each other. Explaining a mechanism while arguing about it, or writing a tutorial in a reflective voice, leaves the reader unsure which parts are fact and which are opinion. The skill flags the clash and offers the nearest pair that works, then does what you ask.
03 · The first draft

The skill writes the first draft.

After four or five questions in one message, the skill writes the whole first draft itself. It does not hand back an empty table for you to fill in. A concrete draft is something you can react to, and any invented visual is marked as a proposal so you know what is still a guess.

04 · The three parts of a scene

The three parts of a scene.

Every scene in the script has three parts. The first two are what the graphic shows and the two to four sentences the reader sees. The third is the transition, the change that happens when the reader scrolls in. People skip that one, and it is where the explanation moves.

05 · Two rules that trim the draft

Two rules that trim the draft.

Two rules cut a first draft down. If the graphic does not change between two scenes, they merge, because a scene with no state change is a paragraph. Each scene shows only what its text is discussing, so the reader keeps reading instead of studying the graphic.

The beat rule has no diagram state of its own — text is subordinate. If a scene needs more than about four sentences, split the concept or move the detail into a static prose section like this one. Mixing plain prose with scroll sequences is the normal shape of these pieces; the scroll blocks are the highlights of a page, not the whole page.
06 · The prose pass

The prose pass.

Each scene runs two to four sentences, which is the length where a draft reaches for a dramatic reveal instead of a sentence. A prose pass removes those shapes before the table is shown, among them colon reveals, binary contrasts, faux-insight openers, and a closing metaphor that stands in for a payoff. It keeps your voice and cuts only the patterns. A dry tone or a stated side comes from the menu, and both survive the pass.

07 · The graphic on a phone

The graphic on a phone.

A desktop layout does not survive a phone by reflowing. The graphic pins to the top of the screen, above the text, and the text scrolls underneath it. Parked behind the cards it disappears, because phone cards are tall enough to cover it. A wide, short screen needs a different layout again, not a smaller band.

The escalation rule. The default output is one standalone HTML file with no build step. React comes in only when the approved script has stateful widgets the reader manipulates, such as filters or a data explorer, and the skill tells you before it makes that call.
08 · Reading it without the scroll

Reading it without the scroll.

The scroll effects are an enhancement, so the article has to work without them. With JavaScript off it reads top to bottom in order. With reduced motion the states still change, they just stop moving. Every meaning lives in the text as well as the graphic, so nothing depends on an animation a reader may never see.

09 · Verified across viewports

Verified across viewports.

The last step drives the built file through a headless browser. It loads the article at nine viewport sizes and asserts the behaviour at each one. It checks for no horizontal overflow, every scene reachable scrolling down and up, and the card sitting clear of the graphic where it settles. The article is done when those checks pass.

The payoff

The reader can describe the method.

The reader can describe the method. Two phases in a fixed order with a gate between them. A script written and approved before any code. Scenes trimmed by the merge and one-concept rules. A prose pass that removes patterns without flattening the voice. A responsive contract that pins the graphic above the text on a phone. And a viewport matrix the built file has to pass. The article is done when those checks pass, not when it looks good on a laptop.
