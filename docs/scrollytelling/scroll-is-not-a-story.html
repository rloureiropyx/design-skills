A product design skill · How scrollytelling works

A page that moves is not a story.

Three minutes of scrolling. One sentence of meaning. Most interactive articles are built animation-first, so the reader arrives at the end impressed and empty-handed. scrollytelling treats the story as the deliverable and the motion as its rendering.

Scroll — the hero is the anti-pattern

The job

Scrollytelling is a writing problem wearing an animation costume.

An agent skill is a set of instructions an AI assistant loads when a request matches it. This one is unusual in that it refuses to start where people ask it to start. Almost every request arrives as “build me an interactive article”, and almost every good one begins as a scene script — closer to how a film is boarded than how a blog post is written.

The reason is structural. In a scroll-driven piece, the reader’s attention is on a rail. What they see, in what order, and what changes underneath them is the argument. Decide that in code and you are directing with a keyboard, discovering the story while fighting the pinning behaviour.

The unit is the scene: one concept, one visual state change. The script is the contract — approved before a line of code — and the piece is finished only when it has been driven across a device matrix, not when it looks good on a laptop.

01 · The shape

Two phases, and a gate between them.

The skill has exactly two phases and they are sequential. Between them sits a gate that only the reader can open. Nothing gets built until the scene script is explicitly approved — and “just build it, I trust you” is the exact pressure the gate exists for.

Why script it like film? Because the reader’s attention is on a rail. A blog post can be reordered after the fact; a scroll sequence cannot, without rebuilding the thing that makes it work.
02 · Phase 1

Four questions, then write the draft.

The skill asks at most four or five questions, in a single message, and only what it cannot infer. Then it writes the whole first draft itself. A concrete draft to argue with beats an empty table — handing back a template to fill in is the failure mode, not the method.

03 · The unit

Visual, text, and the one people forget.

Every scene is defined by three things: what the graphic shows, the two to four sentences the reader sees, and what changes visually on the way in. Transition is not decoration; it is the argument moving. If you cannot say what changes when the reader scrolls in, the scene is not finished.

04 · Beat rule

No state change, no scene.

If the graphic does not change between two adjacent scenes, merge them. A scene without a visual state change is just a paragraph — it did not earn a scroll beat. This single rule removes most of the padding from a first draft, and six strong scenes beat twelve weak ones.

05 · Beat rule

One concept per scene.

What appears on screen must accompany exactly what the text is saying at that moment. Show five things while discussing one and the reader stops reading and starts inspecting — attention leaves the prose and wanders the diagram. Cut the extras, or defer them to the scene that discusses them.

The fourth beat rule has no visual of its own: text is subordinate. If a scene needs more than about four sentences, split the concept or move the detail into a static prose section like this one. Mixing plain prose with scroll sequences is the norm, not a compromise — the scroll blocks are the highlights of a page, not the whole page.
06 · Phase 1

Pick a pattern per sequence.

A diagram evolving in discrete states wants a sticky graphic with steps — the default. A single continuous transformation the reader plays with wants a scrubbed sequence. If neither fits, the scene probably belongs in plain prose, which is a normal outcome and not a retreat.

07 · Approval

The contract closes. Craft stays open.

Approval is the moment the contract closes. From here the implementation follows the approved scenes exactly, in order, with the approved words. Visual polish is still the builder’s job — craft was never up for approval. The boundary fails in both directions, and both failures are quiet.

One escalation rule. The default output is a single standalone HTML file with GSAP from a CDN — no build step, works opened directly. React is used only when the approved script contains stateful widgets the reader manipulates, and the user is told before that decision is taken, not after.
08 · Phase 2

Three ways it breaks on a phone.

Reflowing the grid is not responsive design. The graphic must pin above the text, not behind it, or tall phone cards swallow it entirely. Short viewports need a different pattern, not a smaller band. And a 760-unit diagram in a 350px column runs at 0.46×, so a 14px label lands at six.

09 · Phase 2

Reading it without the scroll.

Scroll effects are progressive enhancement, never the content. The article must read top to bottom with JavaScript off, honour prefers-reduced-motion by switching states instantly rather than removing them, and carry every meaning in text as well as in the graphic. No information may exist only in an animation.

10 · Delivery

Verified, not admired.

The last rule is the least glamorous: do not deliver on visual impression. Drive the file with a headless browser and assert the behaviour at every size — no overflow, every scene reachable both directions, the card clear of the graphic at rest. A piece is finished when it has been proven, not when it has been admired.

What the script produces

One row of the artifact, in full.

This is what a scene looks like before anything is built. Three columns, filled in — and the third is the one that decides whether the piece reads as a story or a slideshow.

Scene 04 · VISUAL

Five scene cards in a strip. Cards 03 and 04 show an identical diagram state and are marked in coral; they fuse into one and the strip re-numbers itself to four.

Scene 04 · TEXTO

“If the graphic does not change state between two scenes, merge them. A scene without a visual state change is just a paragraph — it did not earn a scroll beat.”

Scene 04 · TRANSICIÓN

The single scene card multiplies into the strip; the duplicate pair fuses, and the remaining cards slide left as they re-number. 0.6s, transform and opacity only.

Why the third column decides it

Without it, a builder is left choosing what changes on entry. That choice is the argument, and it is not a rendering detail to be delegated.

What is deliberately absent

No colours, no type scale, no easing curve beyond the duration budget. Those are craft, and craft is not what the reader is being asked to approve.

The payoff

The story was always the deliverable.

Everything in this method exists to stop motion from becoming the point. The gate stops code from renegotiating the story. The merge rule stops scenes from existing to justify scrolling. The device matrix stops a laptop preview from standing in for a phone. What is left, when all of that holds, is an argument the reader can follow with their thumb — and could still follow with the animation switched off. That last part is not an accessibility concession. It is the test of whether there was ever a story there at all.

Method provenance

Where the method comes from.

Three sources, and one deliberate refusal: none of them is a JavaScript tutorial, because the hard part of a scroll piece has never been the JavaScript.

The Pudding and the NYT

The sticky-graphic and step pattern as practised by newsrooms — and the discipline underneath it: scroll sequences are the highlights of a page, not the whole page. Most of a good piece is still prose.

Read The Pudding

GSAP ScrollTrigger

The animation mechanism. Notably, it is not loaded by default here: per-step trigger ranges overlap when a card is shorter than its scene block, so discrete scenes are selected deterministically from scroll position instead.

Read the ScrollTrigger docs

WCAG 2.2

The floor the output has to clear: readable with JavaScript off, prefers-reduced-motion honoured by changing state without motion, and no information conveyed by animation alone.
