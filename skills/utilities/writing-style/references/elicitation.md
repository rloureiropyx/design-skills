# Elicitation

Read this before step 1 of setup. It has the wording for the three evidence paths, the intent
questions, the rules for turning evidence into a rule that will still be true next month, and two
ways of showing the profile back.

## Two inputs, kept apart

A usable profile needs both, and they answer different questions.

**Evidence** — something the person actually wrote or said. This is where rules come from. Personal
style is mostly implicit, so a writer describing their own voice reports what they aspire to rather
than what they do, and demonstrations beat descriptions in every study that has compared them.

**Intent** — what they want to come across as. This is a goal, and asking for it is not the same as
asking someone to describe their style. "How would you describe your writing?" returns an aspiration
wearing the costume of an observation. "What do you want a reader to walk away thinking?" returns a
target, which is useful precisely because it is labelled as one.

Keep them in separate sections and never promote intent to a rule without the user saying so. Step 3
is where the two are compared.

## Path A — samples they already have

The default. Ask once, in one message, and give the reason: people send better samples when they know
what the diversity is for.

> Send me two or three things you've already written — different situations, and about different
> things. A Slack message or email, a paragraph from a doc or a PR description, something you wrote
> for people outside your team. Different subjects matters as much as different formats: if all three
> are about the same project, I end up learning the project instead of learning you.
>
> Rough is better than polished. If you have something you dashed off, send that.

Three things to enforce while collecting:

- **Different subjects.** Content and style entangle in a writer's history. Three samples about the
  same project teach that project's vocabulary and framing.
- **Different registers.** One internal, one external, one somewhere between. A person writing to
  their team and a person writing to a client are recognisably the same writer doing two things, and
  the profile needs both.
- **One per language.** If the user writes in English and Spanish, one sample in each. Rhythm,
  person, hedging, and concreteness transfer across languages. Vocabulary and idiom do not.

Two good samples beat three where one was written to impress.

## Path B — a short exercise

For someone with nothing to hand. Say what it trades, then keep it small:

> Nothing written to hand is fine — two short pieces then, a few sentences each. Fair warning: writing
> on request tends to come out more careful than how you normally write, so this gets us a decent
> start rather than a finished profile. We can sharpen it the first time something I draft doesn't
> sound like you.

The two prompts target two registers rather than two topics:

1. **Explanatory.** *"Explain a decision you made recently to a colleague who wasn't in the room —
   what you chose, and why the obvious alternative was worse."*
2. **Argumentative.** *"Write what you disagree with about a common practice in your field. Say it as
   you would to someone who does it."*

Together they expose the two things a single prompt hides: how the writer explains when nothing is at
stake, and what happens to their sentences when something is.

## Path C — a voice note

For someone who says they do not really write, does not know how they express themselves, or stalls
on Path B. Offer it directly rather than waiting for them to fail the exercise:

> If writing to order feels artificial, talk instead — send me a voice note, or dictate it. Two
> minutes, same two questions. People who don't write much are usually much more themselves out loud,
> and I can work from the transcript.

This is often the better input for a non-writer. Speech carries rhythm, vocabulary, directness,
hedging, what they do instead of a joke, and how they handle disagreement — the parts of voice a
writing task under observation flattens first.

**What a transcript cannot give you**, and what the profile must therefore not claim: punctuation
habits, paragraph structure, formatting, headings, capitalisation. Spoken sentences also run longer
and looser than the same person's written ones, so do not record a sentence-length band from speech
without saying where it came from.

Record the path in the profile. A rule derived from a transcript is still a real rule — it just has a
different reach, and a later reader needs to know that.

## The intent questions

Ask after the evidence, not before. Asking first anchors the samples they choose to send.

> Two quick things about what you're going for — this is separate from how you write today, and I'll
> keep them apart in the profile.
>
> Three or four adjectives for how you'd like to come across. And the one thing you'd hate a reader to
> think about your writing.

**Then push on each adjective once.** An adjective on its own constrains nothing, because every writer
would claim it. What makes it usable is the thing it is being chosen *against*:

> "Direct" — as against blunt, or as against long-winded? Those pull the sentences in opposite
> directions.

Common pairs worth offering when someone is stuck: direct/blunt, warm/casual, rigorous/dense,
confident/salesy, plain/flat, careful/hedged. Someone who cannot generate adjectives can almost always
pick a side of a pair, which is the same information.

Then the three questions from step 2 of the skill. The third — what always or never survives editing —
is the one that produces the overrides list.

## Turning evidence into a rule

Read for what is observable and countable, not for impressions.

| Look at | What it tells you |
| --- | --- |
| Sentence length, and its variation | Whether short sentences are the voice or just the openers. Record a band and the pattern, not an average. |
| First, second, third person | "I think" vs "we found" vs "the data shows" is a stance, and it is consistent per writer far more than per topic. |
| Contractions | A fast read on formality, and one most writers hold steady. |
| Hedging | Whether "probably" and "I think" carry real uncertainty or are throat-clearing. Watch what happens to them under disagreement. |
| Paragraph openers | Whether the point leads or arrives. A habit that holds across topics. |
| Punctuation habits | Em dashes, colons, parentheses, semicolons. Usually unconscious and usually consistent — and a common casualty of an editing pass. *Not available from a transcript.* |
| Concrete vs abstract | Where numbers, names, and examples appear, and where the writer is content to generalise. |
| What stands in for a joke | Understatement, an aside, exaggeration, nothing. Rarely described accurately by the writer, and it survives dictation. |
| Formatting | Headings, bullets, bold, one-line paragraphs. Part of voice in anything written for a screen. *Not available from a transcript.* |

### The rules for a rule

- **Quote a line.** Every observed rule carries a line from the evidence underneath it. A rule with no
  line is intent, and belongs in the intent section.
- **It has to repeat.** A habit shows up across two samples, or several times inside one. One striking
  sentence is a sentence.
- **Write it so it can be broken.** "Prefers short sentences" cannot be applied or checked. "Most
  sentences run 8-18 words; longer ones are built from short clauses joined by commas" can.
- **Three to five signature moves.** More than that and none of them survive contact with a draft.
- **Record what they never do.** Absences are evidence. A writer who never opens with a rhetorical
  question is telling you something a positive rule cannot.
- **Do not claim what the path cannot support.** From a transcript, no punctuation and no formatting
  rules.

### Naming the divergences

Before writing the profile, list every place the intent and the evidence disagree. Each one gets
raised once, in the user's own material:

> You said you want to come across as decisive. In your samples the qualifiers do a lot of work —
> "I think we should probably", "maybe it's worth". When I draft, decisive or as-you-are?

Two or three of these is normal. More than four means the adjectives were collected without the
"compared to what?" follow-up, and are describing an ideal writer rather than this one.

## Deriving the overrides list

While reading, flag anything a generic editing pass would delete that is clearly deliberate here:

- em dashes used often, and used well
- sentence fragments as emphasis
- one-line paragraphs
- opening with "And", "But", "So"
- profanity
- lower-case headings, or no headings at all
- a repeated word the writer refuses to vary

Each one goes in the profile's overrides section. That section out-ranks `slop-check`. Without it,
every pass over the writer's work converges on the same clean, anonymous prose.

Ask the user to confirm the list. This is what the third question was for.

## Showing it back

Two wordings. Pick by whether the person can talk about their own writing.

**When they have samples and opinions**, show the profile as a draft with the quoted lines visible and
ask for the disagreement, not the approval:

> Which of these is wrong? The ones I got from your samples are quoted — argue with the quote if the
> rule doesn't match how you'd describe it.

Do not ask "does this look right?". It gets a yes.

**When they cannot say what is wrong** — the usual case for someone who does not write much — stop
asking them to describe and let them recognise instead. Draft the same short passage two or three ways,
each following one candidate reading of the profile, and ask:

> Here's the same paragraph three ways. Which one sounds most like you? And is there one you'd never
> send?

The rejection is worth as much as the pick, and it is easier to give. Then say what you changed as a
result, so the next round is a smaller question and not the same one again.
