# Craft: What Makes a Diagram Good

From *Stuck? Diagrams Help*, Part 3. There is no universal rulebook — "not much always works" — and the bold words below **must be defined for your specific audience** ("easy," "tidy," "contrast" are all audience-relative). Philosophy: **"There is no right or wrong in diagramming; there is only better and on purpose."** Be **"either consistent, or intentional in your inconsistency."**

Good diagrams are **Accessible · Content-driven · Visually supported** — nine principles across three pillars — and they deliver the five STUCK superpowers: **S**tability, **T**ransparency, **U**nderstanding, **C**larity, **K**indness.

---

## Pillar 1 — ACCESSIBLE
*The access and abilities you provide your audience — and a commitment to designing for abilities different from your own.*

Diagrams are inherently hard for some (visual impairment; some neurodivergent readers). Decide whom you prioritize, and provide alternatives when the diagram can't serve someone.

**Alt text vs. alt content — the decision:** Does the audience need the *spatial* arrangement?
- **Yes** → describe it in **alternative text** (short; include any text in the diagram). The longer the description needs to be, the more you should switch to alt content instead.
- **No** (the *message* matters, not the layout) → provide **alternative content** in another form (large print, audio, Braille, a prose paragraph). Often kinder than long alt text — and writing it usually clarifies the diagram for everyone.

**Principle 1 — Easy to follow.** "Diagrams should not need instruction manuals... should not need tour guides." Give an **obvious starting place** (an over-scaled, contrasting, labeled entry point) and a **strong reading order** matching the audience's cultural/contextual default (left-to-right, top-to-bottom; clockwise reads as forward). Consider the *medium's* reading pattern too (a scrolling page reads top-down). If you feel you must add directional arrows to force an order, the arrangement is probably wrong — rearrange instead.

**Principle 2 — Neat and tidy.** The surest way to cut cognitive load. **Use a grid** to govern alignment, spacing, widths, and placement of headline/key/marginalia. Preflight: (1) lines actually **touch** the shapes they connect; (2) lines and alignment are **straight**; (3) **consistent casing** for labels. **Crossing lines:** don't reach for "bumps" or gaps — usually you can move things so lines don't cross at all; each crossing asks the reader to "hop." When stuck, **duplicate first, then rearrange radically** (swap sides, flip portrait↔landscape, make the linear non-linear, try a metaphor).

**Principle 3 — Contrast aware.** *Contrast is difference that can be perceived* — and the maker's perception ≠ the audience's. Two kinds: **legibility** (element vs. background) and **comparability** (element vs. element, to encode meaning). Contrast is math — measure it against WCAG (SC 1.4.3). Two traps: (a) unintended contrast (inconsistent type/line styles) reads as *meaning* people try to interpret; (b) meaningful contrast that some readers can't perceive. First make intended contrast legible; then remove unintended contrast.

## Pillar 2 — CONTENT DRIVEN
*How content is chosen, framed, and developed. Without good content, no visual can help.*

**Principle 4 — Clear in tone and timescale.** Words + visuals = **tone** (silly or serious — must support the intention). Consistent grammar in labels strengthens tone; a mishmash muddies it. **Timescale** = the period the diagram represents — a critical decision. Don't get stuck between two timescales (mixing "what is" with "what we hope"). Three timescales: **Then** (how it was), **Now** (how it is), **When** (how it could be). Put source timeframes in the key/margins; reinforce timescale in the headline; watch verb tense in labels.

**Principle 5 — Manipulated appropriately.** All diagramming manipulates (from Latin *manipulus*, "handful"). Appropriate manipulation "protects the energy of the audience while keeping them deeply informed on why they can trust" the result. Watch **precision**: avoid *unnecessary exactitude* (3.1% chance of rain) but don't round away meaning where it matters (3.1% of children with concerning lead levels). "Don't water down your data to make it fit. Don't leave it raw just because you like decimal points."

**Principle 6 — Appropriately dense.** Density = ratio of blank space to content; the feel of balance where the eye is "both pleased and rested." No formula exists — squint, trust your gut, then test. Four levers: (1) **amount of content**; (2) **type size & style**; (3) **line & label length**; (4) **leading (line spacing)**. Body copy reads at 45–75 chars/line; **labels should be much shorter — aim under 25 chars.** Leading ~1.5 is often more pleasing than single/double. To cut content: have someone tour the diagram and rank elements 1-2-3 by importance; or ask which element deserves its own "spin-off" diagram and pull it out.

## Pillar 3 — VISUALLY SUPPORTED
*Visuals must **support** the diagram, not decorate it. Intention must not be obfuscated by arbitrary symbols, icons, or color.*

**Principle 7 — Typographically sound.** Choose a typeface for **legibility & readability at your scale and context** (serif or sans — neither is right/wrong). Build a **typographic hierarchy** across: headline, shape labels, line labels, explanatory text, key headline, key content, marginalia. Trick for consistency: style the **longest** label first, then the **shortest**; a scale that serves both serves all. **Label rotation:** (1) try hard **not to rotate at all**; (2) if you must, don't fight the line's edge direction; (3) **never upside-down.** Rotation slows reading ~205%. Set a **minimum type size** for accessibility and edit marginalia down — if it's become tiny novellas, "try harder."

**Principle 8 — Effectively encoded.** Color/pattern/icon can *mean* something, but decoding is the heaviest cognitive tax of all the visual tricks, and color carries cultural baggage (red/green = stop/go in the West, but both auspicious in China; green ≈ death in parts of South America). Red-green color-vision deficiency affects ~1 in 12 men, 1 in 200 women. **Core rule: always back up encoding** — any color/style that means something needs an alternative cue carrying the same info. The most common backup is a **label** (and adding it often makes the color unnecessary). "Just because we can color-code something doesn't mean we should."

**Principle 9 — Thoughtfully embellished.** Embellishment should **enhance** meaning, not obscure it. Images create emotional connection a labeled box can't ("Manatee" box vs. a manatee photo) — but only if the audience will read them (a film icon a child read as bunk beds). Rules: **always label icons** (label them all, then remove the redundant ones); avoid complex iconography that needs a decoder key; don't add embellishment that carries no meaning. But **"less is not always more"** — some contexts need decorative richness to feel like they belong; don't strip out shape, color, texture, and flavor as dogma.

## Diagram Critique BINGO — 25 common violations
Run this before shipping (mapped to the five STUCK superpowers). Any hit = fix it.

1. Contrast ratio issues
2. Unclear timescale
3. Unneeded line jumps used
4. Inappropriate manipulation of data
5. Baseline and edge directions are reversed
6. Data is too precise
7. Not accessible to the intended audience
8. Data is not precise enough
9. Unclear encoding
10. Ineffective or lack of branding
11. Unclear logic or reading direction
12. Tone doesn't match the intention
13. Not tidy
14. Unclear starting place
15. Unclear audience
16. Encoding lacks a backup
17. Too much information
18. Icons lack labels
19. Too much cognitive load for the value delivered
20. Too little information
21. Ineffective use of visual embellishment
22. Labels are too long
23. Not the right information
24. Unclear or ineffective typographic hierarchy
25. Line length and/or line spacing issues

**Done means:** it is a visual representation **and** it helps someone (remember: you count as someone). We can always make a diagram better; we never have to make it perfect.
