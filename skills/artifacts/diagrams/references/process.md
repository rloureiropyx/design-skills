# The Process, In Depth

From *Stuck? Diagrams Help*, Part 2. "There is no one right way to diagram. The following is my way." Treat the eight steps as **training wheels**, not a standard. Learn ingredients and techniques before recipes — "diagramming, like cooking, is more about what the cook does than what the recipe says."

## The emotional arc runs in parallel
People abandon diagramming "the second it gets too hard emotionally." The mechanical process is the antidote — when the emotions feel chaotic, the steps show how far you've come and what's objectively left. Three emotional phases (the "-ings"):

- **Exploring** — researching the audience and setting the intention.
- **Modeling** — deciding scope and scale.
- **Delivering** — actually making it: shapes, lines, labels, key.

Expect dips. Realizing "I need more research / more people / more time before I can do this" at Step 1 is a *useful* outcome, not a failure. The "messy middle" (Step 3) genuinely feels overwhelming — pushing through it is how you reach a finished diagram.

## In-visual elements (invisible, but decide everything)
Left to chance when people jump straight to visuals:
1. **Intention** — the service the diagram delivers; the action/change you hope for.
2. **Audience** — whom it's for; what they know.
3. **Scope** — the boundaries of the content; what you chose *not* to show.

## Visual elements (what the audience sees)
4. **Scale** — how big/small, active/passive the delivery is. 5. **Shapes** — the objects. 6. **Lines** — relationships. 7. **Labels** — verbal+visual markers. 8. **Key** — hints for when meaning isn't self-evident.

## Step 1 — Set an intention
Your unique reason for diagramming *right now*. It becomes the **litmus test for every later decision** ("will this help or hinder my intention?") and the **tiebreaker** when audience research and your gut disagree. Two tests: could a diagram actually do this, and could something *other* than a diagram do it better? Five common intention frames: **reflecting, considering, instructing, identifying, planning.** "Diagramming is not an intention in itself" — sometimes the honest answer is that it should be a conversation, a one-pager, or an email.

## Step 2 — Research your audience
"A diagram is only good in the eye of the beholder." Decide **who** the audience really is — **users** (who navigate the thing) vs. **stakeholders** (who help build it). A nav-hierarchy diagram serves stakeholders, not the website's users — "we can't make the mistake of thinking the diagram is the website." Diagrams for stakeholders-who-serve-users are **work zones, not the final output**, yet must stay truthful to the users' reality. When *you* are stuck, diagramming for yourself is a kindness — Covert's standing question to anyone in a VUCA storm: **"Do you have a diagram of it yet?"**

**Research methods (rightsize — not every diagram needs all six):**
- **Qualitative interviewing** (Exploring) — 1-on-1 about the *subject*, not the diagram. Don't announce "I'm making a diagram"; share your *intention*. It's "a job interview for the diagram."
- **Draw & share** (Exploring) — ask people to draw from a prompt, then compare; surfaces fraught terminology and mental tangles.
- **Collaborative diagramming** (→ Modeling) — watch your audience diagram *with each other* to learn how they model. **Not** "diagram by committee" toward a final artifact.
- **Card sorts** (Modeling) — open (they make categories) or closed (you provide them); listen to *why* they group and label things.
- **Observational usability testing** (Delivering) — the only way to know it makes sense: ask someone to **give you a tour** of it. Show it **without explaining** — "if it can't project its own intention, no amount of explaining will help." State your intention for the *session*, not for the *diagram*. Question isn't "did they like it?" but **"can they use it as intended?"** Don't fix it in front of them.
- **A/B testing** (Delivering) — only when torn between two directions. *Preference:* show both to the same people (alternate order). *Effectiveness:* one version per group, everything else held constant.

## Step 3 — Choose your scope (the messy middle)
Fill in the Mad-Lib: *"To meet my intention, the diagram will cover ___ and will not cover ___."* Three failures: **too much** (cognitive load — interrogate every element, "diagrams need to breathe"), **too little** (missing sources/timeframes; unexplained icons/acronyms — list the questions the audience arrives with), **not the right information** (wrong register/vocabulary — the Goldilocks failure, a sign intention or audience work is incomplete). "The only way to know is to test the diagram with your audience."

## Step 4 — Determine scale
Scale = **size × interaction mode.** Reject naïve heuristics ("more space = more content"; "bigger = bolder"). The 2×2: **large-active** (museum wall you approach) · **large-passive** (billboard driven past) · **small-active** (workbook written in) · **small-passive** (field guide referred to). Scale can change *how many* diagrams and *how many* mediums you need. This step often triggers the deepest emotional dip — lots of hard thinking with nothing that looks like a diagram yet. Normal.

## Steps 5 & 6 — Set shapes, add lines (a two-step dance)
**Shapes = objects** (people, places, things). **Lines = relationships.** Do them together: move shapes into arrangements and *imagine* the lines; "if you do that part right, the lines almost draw themselves." Don't commit lines until the structure is right — otherwise you redo everything.

**What shape?** (order matters — each in its ideal context): decision → **diamond**; multiples → **stack** (affinities / instances / equivalents; collapsed = "many," spread = one thing's many labels); lengthy label → **rectangle/square** (most forgiving — the beginner default); getting fancy → stars/icons/illustrations (last resort, test comprehension); otherwise **circle/oval** (plain, softer, less forgiving of long labels). Beginner rule: "just use diamonds, stacks, rectangles, and squares. There is no shame in that game." Fancy shapes without tested comprehension "become decoration, not information."

**How you place shapes already communicates** two things at once: **reading order** and **relationships**. The five common arrangements:
1. **This is part of that** → nest smaller shapes inside a larger one.
2. **This is a type of that** → indent children under a parent, connect with brackets.
3. **This leads to that** → arrows (available directions imply unavailable ones).
4. **This relates to that** → a plain line; **usually needs a label**; vary weight/style for strength (solid = close, dotted = loose).
5. **This is the same as that** → overlap; essential for collecting others' equivalent terms.

**Line patterns → relationship types:** non-directional → **association** (label it); one-way → **sequence/flow** (also used as pointers and axes); two-way → **reciprocation/equivalence**; brackets → **hierarchy** (horizontal for the top ~2 levels, **switch to vertical at 3+** rather than adding color-codes); quadrant position → **comparison**.

## Step 7 — Refine labels
Labels evolve by phase: **to get by** (Exploring — don't agonize; "there is relief in calling something by a word you know you'll change"), **to get through** (Modeling — pressure them to make sense to others), **to get results** (Delivering — they must **speak for themselves** and **match the intention**). Verify by having an audience member present the diagram back. Labels are usually the last adjustments made.

## Step 8 — Add a key (only what's needed)
You don't always need a "Key" box; you *do* need every element to be self-evident. A key legitimately holds: (1) the **visual vocabulary** (why some shapes/lines differ), (2) **citations, timeframes, methods**, (3) a note of **intention** (how to read it / the takeaway). A key must never rescue three things — each a sign the *diagram* is underperforming: overloaded color/icon coding ("lazy diagramming," decoder rings), cryptically labeled elements (A/B/C forcing decode — if people rewrite labels to avoid the key, you have this problem; fix by labeling everything, then scaling back), or dumped research (nobody wants your rabbit holes; it triggers "diagrammatic insecurity" that tells the reader "this isn't for you").

## Recurring maxims
- **Intention is the through-line and tiebreaker** for every decision.
- **Cognitive load is the enemy** — added by too much info, overloaded keys, and forced decoding.
- **Diagrams need to breathe** — blank space is a feature.
- **Fix the structure; don't pile on encoding.**
- **Set shapes before lines; let lines almost draw themselves.**
- **"The only way to know is to test the diagram with your audience"** — the single most-repeated rule.
- **"There is no one right way."**
