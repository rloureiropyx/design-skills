# Brown's Diagram Craft (Communicating Design, Ch. 1–2)

Dan Brown's web/product-design layer, complementing Covert. Where Covert gives the general craft and the recipes, Brown adds structure, a drawing process, state conventions, and stakeholder presentation.

## Diagrams vs. deliverables (the hinge)

- **Diagram = "parts of the story."** A single artifact (a flow, a sitemap, a wireframe). "It may be entertaining and evocative on its own, but it comes with no context."
- **Deliverable = "the whole story."** A stand-alone document that wraps diagrams in context, rationale, and next steps. In this plugin that document is a `proposal`; a `deliverables` skill, if installed, covers the wider document family.
- Documents are **"catalysts and by-products"** — never the objective. **"Plan activities, not deliverables"**: choose work by its ability to reach an objective, not because you want to produce a particular artifact.

## The three layers of a diagram

Importance tiers (not physical layers). Build in order; stop when the intention is met.
- **Layer 1 — Essentials.** "If any of this is absent, you would not even call the artifact by the same name." (A sitemap without pages isn't a sitemap.)
- **Layer 2 — Elaboration.** Detail on the essentials; remove it and "your artifact would still be valid but somewhat less meaningful." Don't use every element.
- **Layer 3 — Context & connection.** Dates, launch status, links to other artifacts.
- **Why layers:** an antidote to overload. "When in doubt, do Layer 1 only," then add one or two Layer-2 elements as confidence grows. "As you add more information, it becomes easier to obscure the main message."

## Decide before drawing

- **Purpose** — every artifact needs its own (capture current state / establish the design problem / present ideas).
- **Audience** — beyond role: detail-oriented vs. big-picture, abstract vs. concrete, planners vs. jump-in, text vs. visual.
- **Content — the three A's:** **Abstraction** (how specific?), **Analysis** (what did close looking reveal?), **Agenda** (what will move the project now?).

## A rough process for diagramming (10 steps)

1. List the content. 2. **Sketch first** (pen and paper). 3. **Share early** ("backseat your ego"). 4. Iterate. 5. **Explore a different approach** — turn your assumptions on their head. 6. Move stuff around (esp. node-and-link). 7. **Color sparingly** — "when in doubt, stick with varying shades of gray." 8. **Use conventions** — refine your visual language, strip visual noise. 9. Revisit inputs (check against requirements/research). 10. **"Label, label, label" — "you can never have too much labeling."**

Common traps: lack of planning; **"no narrative"** ("a polite way of saying 'hubris'" — a diagram delivered outside a document is "a very special, very extreme case"); too much information.

## State conventions (as-is / to-be / to-be-eventually)

A recurring scope problem is mixing states. Handle it with a **progression** of drawings, or a **consistent visual convention** for state (e.g., gray = current, dark = new). Other boundary problems: *detail vs. complexity* (sequence detail-people and big-picture-people); *product vs. context* (draw the line "between what you can influence and what you cannot"; acknowledge risks at the edges).

## Presenting (shared recipe)

Brown's 8-step meeting recipe, summarised in `SKILL.md` → "Presenting & collaborating": context → conventions → major decisions → rationale/constraints → details via **PGP: particular–general–particular** → implications → feedback as **Aspects + Questions** → framework for review. If a `communicate-design` skill is installed, the long form lives in its `references/review-facilitation.md`. *"You are a journalist, not a joke-teller"*: land the main message first. Design **principles** are "a super-set of all other constraints" and must be specific, not "make it easy to use." Get feedback on *the ideas*, not the artifact. **"Leave your ego at the door."**

## Covert ↔ Brown reconciliation

They agree far more than they conflict (purpose-first, audience-is-everything, guard scope, shapes-then-lines, label discipline, color restraint, iterate/test, diagram-as-conversation). Resolve the four apparent tensions by **audience / scale / stage**:

1. **Keys.** Covert: a diagram must be self-evident; a key must never be a decoder ring. Brown: keys are tolerable because a human narrates. → **Self-evident for async/standalone; narrator-supported keys acceptable in a live-presented deliverable.**
2. **Labeling.** Brown: "you can never have too much labeling" (while drafting). Covert: short labels, finalized last (at delivery). → **Over-label while drafting; tighten at delivery.**
3. **Recipes vs. "better and on purpose."** → Covert's voice governs *whether/what* to draw; Brown's patterns govern *how to package and present* it.
4. **Standalone diagrams.** Covert: a helpful standalone diagram is a valid goal. Brown: a diagram outside a document is "hubris." → **Standalone is fine for quick/exploratory work; wrap it in a deliverable when it crosses to stakeholders.**
